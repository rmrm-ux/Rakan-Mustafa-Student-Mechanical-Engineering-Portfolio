DWG NO. 01 EV Battery Module 1D Thermal Solver & Cooling System Optimization

EV Battery Thermal Solver

Built a 1D transient finite-difference solver in MATLAB to optimize lithium-ion battery cooling architecture, reducing peak fast-charging temperatures by 74.8% and eliminating cell-to-cell thermal gradients.

To solve severe thermal bottlenecking during aggressive 35A DC fast-charging in a 10-cell 21700 battery module, I built a 1D transient thermal solver in MATLAB using the Explicit Finite Difference Method (Euler Forward Integration). The model accounts for energy conservation, thermal resistance networks, and temperature-dependent internal resistance (R-int). Validation revealed that the initial End-Cooled Architecture (Phase 1) choked heat transfer, causing a catastrophic 130°C meltdown and a 100°C gradient across the module. By re-architecting the system into a Bottom Chill Plate System (Phase 2), every cell was given an independent cooling path—capping peak temperatures at a safe 32.7°C (well under the 45°C limit) and reducing cell-to-cell gradients to 0°C. For future iterations, I plan to expand this model into a 3D CFD analysis to model coolant fluid temperature rise across the flow channels.

DWG NO. 02 Dual-Disc Cycloidal Drive — Mechanical Design & Kinematic Validation

Dual-Disc Cycloidal Drive

Designed and modeled a 10:1 speed-reduction cycloidal drive from first principles — parametric CAD profile generation, dual-disc dynamic balancing, and full kinematic validation via SolidWorks Motion Study.

Status: Design complete, kinematics validated in simulation. Physical build (3D-printed prototype) and empirical friction/stress testing in progress — physical validation photos and data to follow.

The project spans Mechanical design, applied mathematics, CAD-driven manufacturing constraints, and mechatronic system integration — combining a mathematically derived disc geometry with a fully constrained, motion-validated assembly before committing to fabrication.

Key Engineering Contributions

Parametric profile generation from governing equations — rather than relying on freeform or AI-generated spline approximations, the cycloidal disc profile was derived directly from the standard epicycloidal equation and generated as an exact point-table (720 points per revolution), imported into SolidWorks via Curve Through XYZ Points. This ensured a mathematically accurate tooth profile with a defined, defensible design basis — every dimension traces back to four governing parameters: lobe count (N = 10), pin circle radius (Rz = 29.5 mm), eccentricity (e = 1.5 mm), and ring pin radius (rz = 2 mm).

Dual-disc balanced configuration — to cancel the inherent eccentric imbalance force of a single-disc cycloidal reducer, the design uses two identical disc profiles mounted 180° out of phase on a common eccentric shaft. This required independently deriving and validating a second, rotationally-offset profile and re-establishing correct kinematic mates (gear mates, parallel constraints) between each disc and the output carrier — a non-trivial constraint-resolution problem solved through iterative CAD debugging and interference analysis.

Reduction ratio and drivetrain — 10 lobes / 11 ring pins yield a 10:1 reduction between the input and disc orbit, with a 1:1 coupling from disc to output pin carrier via oversized output holes (pin diameter + 2× eccentricity clearance), correctly sized to accommodate the disc's orbital path without binding. The system was modeled using SolidWorks Gear Mates to enforce correct relative angular velocity and direction between the input shaft, discs, and output carrier.

Design-for-manufacture considerations — sourced a standard, off-the-shelf tolerance stack (4 mm ring pins, 5 mm output pins, 20 mm/32 mm 6804-series bearings at the eccentric-to-disc interface) to avoid non-standard or 3D-printed bearing surfaces at rolling-contact interfaces and preserve accurate friction test data. Verified geometry using SolidWorks Interference Detection across full rotation cycles, catching and correcting a false-clipping condition and a genuine shaft/carrier bore interference before committing to print. Selected a NEMA 17 stepper motor with an A4988/DRV8825 driver for Arduino-based closed-loop control and step-accurate input rotation logging, enabling planned instrumented friction and torque testing.

Validation

Kinematic behavior — including correct 10:1 reduction, opposite-direction disc rotation relative to the input, and synchronized output carrier motion — was verified using a SolidWorks Motion Study with a constant-speed rotary motor applied to the input shaft, confirming the assembly's gear mates and constraint scheme produce physically correct motion prior to physical fabrication.

Next Steps

  • 3D-print the full assembly (housing, dual discs, output carrier) at the UMD makerspace
  • Assemble with sourced hardware (bearings, dowel pins, motor, driver)
  • Conduct instrumented friction and stress testing using Arduino-based rotation/torque logging
  • Publish empirical results alongside simulation predictions

DWG NO. 03 Front Splitter Aerodynamic Analysis (CFD)

Front Splitter Aerodynamic Analysis (CFD)

Taught myself CFD from scratch in SolidWorks Flow Simulation by running a full external-flow wind tunnel test on a front splitter I'd modeled freshman year — catching and fixing a fluid-density bug along the way, and validating the result to convergence before trusting any of the numbers.

I originally modeled this front splitter during winter break of my freshman year, just as a design exercise. Coming back to it now, I wanted to actually simulate how it performs — and use it as a way to teach myself computational fluid dynamics from scratch in SolidWorks Flow Simulation, a tool I'd never touched before.

I set up an external flow wind-tunnel test around the splitter at 20 m/s (about 45 mph), targeting two outcomes: a surface pressure distribution across the part, and a set of flow trajectory streamlines showing how air deflects around the leading edge and underneath the undertray.

Test Setup

  • Analysis type — External flow, steady-state
  • Fluid — Air (default SolidWorks properties, ~1.2 kg/m³)
  • Freestream velocity — 20 m/s (~45 mph)
  • Ambient conditions — 101,325 Pa, 293.2 K
  • Flow regime — Laminar and turbulent (SolidWorks default)

Debugging & Troubleshooting

Since this was fully self-taught, most of the real work was figuring out why things were wrong before I could trust anything the solver told me:

Diagnosing a fluid setup error — my first run came back with pressure values off by roughly three orders of magnitude. Tracing it back, I found the project's default fluid was set to water instead of air — a mistake that became obvious once I ran the numbers myself: dynamic pressure at 20 m/s should land around 245 Pa, not the tens of thousands I was seeing. That sanity check is what caught it, not the solver flagging anything.

Working through boundary conditions — I had to learn the difference between internal and external flow analysis types, why boundary conditions apply to the domain rather than the model surface in external flow, and how to correctly define global velocity through Initial and Ambient Conditions.

Catching CAD/mesh quality issues — a geometry check turned up sliver faces, short edges, and knife vertices left over from the import process, which were locally corrupting the pressure solution near the leading edge. Rather than pretending they weren't there, I worked around them — manually excluding the flagged faces from the surface pressure plot instead of trusting the raw output.

Verifying convergence — I tracked Maximum Velocity and Force (Y and Z) goals across iterations to confirm the solution had actually stabilized before treating any results as valid. All three goals flattened out by around iteration 20–30 of the ~58 total, which is what let me trust the final numbers instead of just assuming a completed run meant a correct one.

Results

By the end of the run, the goal plots showed clean, flat convergence:

  • Maximum velocity converged to ~23–24 m/s — consistent with freestream plus local acceleration around the part
  • Force (Y) converged to roughly 250–300 N
  • Force (Z) converged to roughly -1,000 N, in the direction consistent with downforce for this coordinate setup

Qualitatively, the flow trajectories show visible curvature and upwash near the splitter's leading edge — air deflecting up and over, exactly what you'd expect from a splitter shape. The surface pressure plot backs that up: elevated stagnation pressure at the leading edge, and reduced pressure along the underside, consistent with downforce-generating behavior.

Caveats

This was intentionally a simplified, illustrative aero study — not a production CFD workflow. I was teaching myself the tool and validating a design concept, not certifying a part for use.

  • The imported CAD had minor geometry defects (sliver faces, short edges, knife vertices from the import) that I identified through a geometry analysis. I made a deliberate choice not to fully repair them, and instead worked around them — manually excluding the flagged faces from the surface plot — rather than pretending they weren't there.
  • Because goals integrate pressure over the entire body surface (including those flagged faces), the Force Y/Z numbers likely carry more error than the surface pressure plot does. I'd treat them as order-of-magnitude, not precise — the trend and general magnitude are physically reasonable, but I wouldn't quote them to two significant figures without cleaning up the geometry first.

This project was less about the specific part and more about proving to myself I could take a CFD tool I'd never used, break it, figure out why it broke, and still land on a physically sound, converged result on my own.
