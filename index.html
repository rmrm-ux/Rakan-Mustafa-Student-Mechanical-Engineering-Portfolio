DWG NO. 01 EV Battery Module 1D Thermal Solver & Cooling System Optimization

EV Battery Thermal Solver

Built a 1D transient finite-difference solver in MATLAB to optimize lithium-ion battery cooling architecture, reducing peak fast-charging temperatures by 74.8% and eliminating cell-to-cell thermal gradients.

To solve severe thermal bottlenecking during aggressive 35A DC fast-charging in a 10-cell 21700 battery module, I built a 1D transient thermal solver in MATLAB using the Explicit Finite Difference Method (Euler Forward Integration). The model accounts for energy conservation, thermal resistance networks, and temperature-dependent internal resistance (R-int). Validation revealed that the initial End-Cooled Architecture (Phase 1) choked heat transfer, causing a catastrophic 130°C meltdown and a 100°C gradient across the module. By re-architecting the system into a Bottom Chill Plate System (Phase 2), every cell was given an independent cooling path—capping peak temperatures at a safe 32.7°C (well under the 45°C limit) and reducing cell-to-cell gradients to 0°C. For future iterations, I plan to expand this model into a 3D CFD analysis to model coolant fluid temperature rise across the flow channels.

DWG NO. 02 Dual-Disc Cycloidal Drive — Mechanical Design & Kinematic Validation

Dual-Disc Cycloidal Drive

Designed and modeled a 10:1 speed-reduction cycloidal drive from first principles — parametric CAD profile generation, dual-disc dynamic balancing, and full kinematic validation via SolidWorks Motion Study.

Status: Design complete, kinematics validated in simulation. Physical build (3D-printed prototype) and empirical friction/stress testing in progress — physical validation photos and data to follow.

The project spans mechanism design, applied mathematics, CAD-driven manufacturing constraints, and mechatronic system integration — combining a mathematically derived disc geometry with a fully constrained, motion-validated assembly before committing to fabrication.

Key Engineering Contributions

Parametric profile generation from governing equations — rather than relying on freeform or AI-generated spline approximations, the cycloidal disc profile was derived directly from the standard epicycloidal equation and generated as an exact point-table (720 points per revolution), imported into SolidWorks via Curve Through XYZ Points. This ensured a mathematically accurate tooth profile with a defined, defensible design basis — every dimension traces back to four governing parameters: lobe count (N = 10), pin circle radius (Rz = 29.5 mm), eccentricity (e = 1.5 mm), and ring pin radius (rz = 2 mm).

Dual-disc balanced configuration — to cancel the inherent eccentric imbalance force of a single-disc cycloidal reducer, the design uses two identical disc profiles mounted 180° out of phase on a common eccentric shaft. This required independently deriving and validating a second, rotationally-offset profile and re-establishing correct kinematic mates (gear mates, parallel constraints) between each disc and the output carrier — a non-trivial constraint-resolution problem solved through iterative CAD debugging and interference analysis.

Reduction ratio and drivetrain — 10 lobes / 11 ring pins yield a 10:1 reduction between the input and disc orbit, with a 1:1 coupling from disc to output pin carrier via oversized output holes (pin diameter + 2× eccentricity clearance), correctly sized to accommodate the disc's orbital path without binding. The system was modeled using SolidWorks Gear Mates to enforce correct relative angular velocity and direction between the input shaft, discs, and output carrier.

Design-for-manufacture considerations — sourced a standard, off-the-shelf tolerance stack (4 mm ring pins, 5 mm output pins, 20 mm/32 mm 6804-series bearings at the eccentric-to-disc interface) to avoid non-standard or 3D-printed bearing surfaces at rolling-contact interfaces and preserve accurate friction test data. Verified geometry using SolidWorks Interference Detection across full rotation cycles, catching and correcting a false-clipping condition and a genuine shaft/carrier bore interference before committing to print. Selected a NEMA 17 stepper motor with an A4988/DRV8825 driver for Arduino-based closed-loop control and step-accurate input rotation logging, enabling planned instrumented friction and torque testing.

Validation

Kinematic behavior — including correct 10:1 reduction, opposite-direction disc rotation relative to the input, and synchronized output carrier motion — was verified using a SolidWorks Motion Study with a constant-speed rotary motor applied to the input shaft, confirming the assembly's gear mates and constraint scheme produce physically correct motion prior to physical fabrication.

  • Assemble with sourced hardware (bearings, dowel pins, motor, driver)

  • Publish empirical results alongside simulation predictions
